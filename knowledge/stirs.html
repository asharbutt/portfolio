Short Term Interest Rate Futures

Short term interest rate futures, or STIRs for short, are contracts designed to allow for speculation and hedging on interest rates. Just like an FRA, two parties enter a contract to exchange a floating rate vs a fixed rate, agreed on the start of the contract. The idea is the same as an FRA,however, there are quite a few differences. STIRs are exchange traded contracts, and come in spefications in order to increase liquidity. As they are exhcange traded, they require initial and variational margin, and profits/losses are settled daily.

The daily settlement reduces counterparty credit exposure, but also allows for speculators to leverage heavily, as an initial margin deposit usually allows you to enter a large notional contract. The value of a STIR is usually calculated as 100-R, where R is the interest rate.

Futures measure the cost of financing, as going long on a future means you are going short on the floating rate i.e. receiving the fixed rate. This is the equivalent to a fixed deposit. Going short futures means you are paying the fixed rate, and receving the float, equivalent of a fixed loan.

Futures are all cash settled, meaning the position is marked to market daily, and is therefore settled on a daily basis. Given that they are used for speculation and hedging, most futures contract dont actually expire, and are either closed out or rolled into the next expiry.

We can break down the differences between FRAs and Futures:

  • 1. FRAs are OTC/bilateral, meaning no requirement for Initial or Variational Margin (though this can be provided to reduce CVA costs), Futures are exchange traded with both margins required. This increase the counterparty credit risk exposure for FRAs, but reduces it for Futures
  • 2. FRAs are bespoke, meaning all contract parameters can be adjusted, Futures come in pre-specified contracts, meaning there can be a hedging mismatch if the client has a bespoke requirement
  • 3. Due to FRAs being OTC and more bespoke, they are not as liquid, and can be difficult and costly to close out, whereas given Futures are on exchange, they are cheap to enter, easy to close out and usually have tighter bid/ask spreads

Futures are quoted by their price (100-R), and their contract specification will have a tick size and tick value. The tick size is the smallest measurement in the change of the futures contract. For SOFR futures, the smallest tick size is 1 basis point. This makes it easier for margin calculations as well.

The tick value will then be calculated as follows:

$$Tick Value = Tick size * Daycount(T_{1}, T_{2})*Notional$$

Convexity Adjustment

The payoff of an FRA is convex, meaning the price is a convex function of the interest rate. Meanwhile, the futures payoff is linear. The FRA has a discounting component to it due to the lack of immediate settlement, and this discounting component moves with the interest rates. These adjustment affect risk calculations (such as margin reinvestment or exposure), and can change the pricing or quotation on the products, leading to a bias

When constructing yield curve, the aim is to produce unadjusted discount factors and forward rates, meaning we have to remove any form of currency, funding or timing adjustments. FRAs by definition do not contain any funding adjustments, and so the forward rates tend to be natural (unless some CVA adjustment is applied), however, futures having daily settlement, and thus there is a margining effect, which contributes to the linearity of the futures price. Therefore, when using futures to build the short end of the yield curve, you have to remove this margin adjustment from the futures rate in order to align it with the forward rate from an FRA. This is known as the convexity adjustment.
