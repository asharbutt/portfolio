Heston Volatility

Introduction

Black Scholes model assumes that the volatility is constant across all strikes and maturities. The implied volatilities from the market say otherwise, and show that there is a skew present. The Local Volatility model is great for vanilla options as it is able to replicate and reproduce the market implied surface perfectly, given it is just a transformation of the surface. However, if we are to simulate some option prices to the future, and then imply the volatility back towards another point in the future (such as a forward start option), we note that the implied skew is actually much flatter than what we would expect. This is a limitation of the local vol mdoel, and thus it is not a good vol model to use for products which rely on forward skews.

We therefore introduce a stochastic volatility model under which the variance process itself is stochastic. We start of with the Schobel-Zhu process, which is the adapted Ohrnstein-Uhlenbeck process from interest rates:

$$\boxed{\begin{cases} dS(t) = rS(t)dt + \sigma S(t) dW \\ d\sigma (t) = \kappa (\bar{\sigma} - \sigma)dt + \gamma dW \end{cases}}$$

Where $\kappa$ is the speed of mean reversion, $\bar{\sigma}$ is the long run volatility and $\gamma$ is the vol of vol. The issue with this process is that it allows for the volatility process to go negative, which cannot happen. We therefore introduce the CIR process, which avoids negative vols under a Feller condition:

$$\boxed{\begin{cases} d\nu (t) = \kappa (\bar{\nu} - \nu)dt + \gamma \sqrt{\nu} dW \end{cases}}$$

Here, we model the variance rather than the volatility, and having $\sqrt{\nu}$ in the process avoids the process entering negative territory. The Feller condition ($2\kappa \bar{\nu} \geq \gamma ^{2}$) guarantees that the variance will not become negative. If this condition is not satisied, then the CDF will exhibit a massive spike near the origin, i.e. the left tail of the density will grow extremely fast around 0.

We then introduce the Heston Stochastic Vol Model below:

$$\boxed{\begin{cases} dS(t) = rS(t)dt + \sqrt{\nu} S(t) dW_{S}^{Q} \\ d\nu (t) = \kappa (\bar{\nu} - \nu)dt + \gamma \sqrt{\nu} dW_{\nu}^{Q} \end{cases}}$$

Change of Measure from real world to risk-neutral

We begin with the real world probability process:

$$\boxed{\begin{cases} dS(t) = rS(t)dt + \sqrt{\nu} S(t) dW_{S}^{P} \\ d\nu (t) = \kappa (\bar{\nu} - \nu)dt + \gamma \sqrt{\nu} dW_{\nu}^{P} \end{cases}}$$

Where $dW_{S}^{P}, dW_{\nu}^{P}$ are two correlated Brownian motions with $dW_{S}^{P}dW_{\nu}^{P} = \rho_{S,\nu}dt$. We know that the Cholesky decomposed correlation matrix applied to the uncorrelated Brownian motions to arrive at the correlated processes is as follows:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \rho_{S, \nu} & \sqrt{1-\rho_{S, \nu}^2} \end{pmatrix}$$

Recall that the correlated Brownian motion can then be written as the following vector (which is just the decomposed correlation matrix above multiplied by a vector of the two independent brownian motions):

$$\begin{pmatrix} d\sim{W}_{S}^{P} \\ \rho_{S, \nu} d\sim{W}_{S}^{P} + d\sim{W}_{\nu}^{P}\sqrt{1-\rho_{S, \nu}^2} \end{pmatrix}$$
