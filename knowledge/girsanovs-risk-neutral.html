Radon-Nikodym Derivative and Change of measure

We start by introducing the Radon-Nikodym derivative. Lets assume we have two measures Q and P, and these two are absolutely mutually continious with respect to each other. This means $Q \gg P$ and $P \gg Q$, which in other words means that for all sets A where P(A)=0, Q(A)=0, and for all sets where Q(A)=0, P(A)=0 as well.

Simply speaking, for any set A where the probability under one measure is 0, the other also agrees that the probability is 0. Both measures will agree on which sets have a probability 0. For all other sets where the probability is non-zero, both sets will asign a probability, which doesnt have to be the same.

If these two measures are absolutely continious with respect to each other, then there exists some variable Z that will describe the ratio between the change of these two measures. We can write this ratio as:

$$Z = \frac{dQ}{dP}$$

For this to hold, EZ = 1. We can then define expectations of variables under the two measures using Z:

$$E^{Q}[X] = E^{P}[XZ], E^{P}[X]=E^{Q}[\frac{X}{Z}]$$

Girsanovs Theorem

The idea of Girsanovs simply put is: If we want to change the drift of a process, then we can do so by changing the probabilities assigned to the space of outcomes associated with the process by changing the measure.

For our options case, we start off with the SDE under the real world measure: $dS = \mu S dt + \sigma S dW^{P}$. We are interested in evaluating the payoff max(S-K,0) in a risk neutral world, where the drift is not $\mu$, but rather r. We cant just do this by switching in r, because the probabilities for $dW^{P}$ are still under a different measure. We define an arbitrary function as the market price of risk, $\theta = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma}$. For changing the measure, we always define a Dolean Dade exponential, which is always a local martingale:

$$Z = e^{-\int _{0}^{t}\theta (u)dW(u) - \frac{1}{2} \int _{0}^{t} \theta ^{2}du}$$

This will always be a local martingale, but the choice of $\theta$ will decide whether Z is a true martingale. Given that $Z_{0}=1$, then we would need EZ=1. In order for this to be true, we need $Z_{0}=1$ (which always holds), and we also need to fulfil some conidtion (such as Novikovs) to ensure EZ=1. Novikovs states:

$$E[e^{\frac{1}{2}\int _{0}^{t}\theta ^{2}(s)ds}]< \infty$$

Another simpler condition is: $E[\int _{0}^{t} \theta ^{2}(u)Z^{2}du] < \infty$. Both conditions can ensure EZ=1, and either is sufficient to prove so.

If we have a suitable $\theta (u)$ such that EZ=1, then Girsanovs states that the following is then a new Brownian motion under the new measure:

$$W^{Q} = W^{P} + \int _{0}^{t} \theta (u) du$$

We can then substitute this into our SDE under the original measure (the real world measure) and make the change to the new measure. We can do the following to our GBM:

$$dS = \mu S dt + \sigma S dW^{P}$$

$$dS = \mu S dt + \sigma S (dW^{Q} - \theta dt)$$

$$dS = \mu S dt + \sigma S (dW^{Q} - \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma} dt)$$

$$dS = \mu S dt + \sigma S dW^{Q} - \mu S dt + r S dt$$

$$dS = r S dt + \sigma S dW^{Q}$$

And with that, this SDE describes the evolution of the stock price under the risk neutral measure. This means that we can now evaluate our payoff under the risk neutral measure using the solution to this SDE to determine the distribution of the asset price.

Under this measure, the discounted stock price (the bank account is the numeraire, the discount factor is when we move the numeraires around in the expectation) is a martingale. Again, we also want to be sure that with our choice of $\theta$, the numeraire adjusted asset process is also a martingale

Finding the market price of risk adjustment

We know the asset price process under the real world measure is : $dS = \mu S dt + \sigma S dW^{P}$, and the discount factor follows the process: $dB = -rBdt$. We know this because the discount factor can be written as: $B = e^{-\int _{0}^{t}r(s)ds}$, and this is similar to our solution to the geometric Brownian motion, hence we know the SDE is also geometric.
