Delta

One way of pricing an option is by creating a hedging portfolio which hedges the payoff of the option. You can hedge delta by taking a position in the underlying in the opposite direction. A stock (or any underlying) will have a delta of 1 - this makes sense as the movement in the stock with respect to the stock itself is always 1. In the famous Black Scholes equation, the way to do this is by creating a delta hedged portfolio, which consists of an option and shorting Delta amounts of the stock. This makes the overall portfolio hedged against any small movements in the underlying. We should this via: $\Pi = V - \Delta S$.

The Delta shows us the change in the Option Value in the currency amount (e.g. Dollars) for a change in the underlying, and ranges between 0 and 1 for Calls, and -1 and 0 for puts. We can calculate the delta using the Black Scholes formula, where we take the first derivative with respect to the underlying:

$$\frac{\partial V_{Call}}{\partial S} = e^{-qT}N(d_{1})$$

We observe that the Delta for the call option is close to 0 when deep OTM, and close to 1 when deep ITM. As we get closer to the maturity, we see that the Delta approaches its relative values (0 for OTM, 1 for ITM), and the range where the delta is actually changing tightens up around the ATM. This makes sense as we have lost all time value, and the option price reflects mostly the intrinsic value, and if you were OTM close to expiry, then your option will not carry much intrinsic value, and neither will it experience a change in intrinsic value unless you are sitting on the ATM point.

The delta is important as a trader aims to hedge his option positions, usually on a book level. When setting up the hedging portfolio, the cost of the hedge will reflect the cost of the option. Under Black-scholes, it is assumed that the option is hedged continiously, with no friction i.e. liquidity issues, no transaction costs, perfect information. In reality, these factors play an important part in how costly it is to delta hedge. For example, thin liquidity can make shorting/buying shares more expensive, thus the cost of setting up the hedging portfolio is higher, and so this cost would be reflected in the option premium.

Black Scholes assumes the hedging is continious, but in reality continious hedging is impossible. Given transaction costs and not being able to transact continiously, traders will look to rebalance their hedge portfolio discretely. The frequency depends on how accurate the hedge needs to be, with daily rebalancing being quite common. The lower your frequency, the more your hedge will move out of line with the portfolio, but the more common the hedging frequency, the more you will pay to rebalance in transaction costs.

Factors affecting Delta

Time to Maturity

We have already observed the effect of time to maturity on the Delta - as we get closer to maturity/expiry, the option loses its time value and reflects more and more of its intrinsic value.

Volatility

Increased volatility gives a higher chance of the underlying moving towards the strike, meaning a higher probability of being ITM. This means that options that are OTM would have a higher probability of becoming ITM, and thus the impact on those option prices would be higher. For ITM options, this is the reverse, whilst yes the underlying can move even deeper ITM, there is a possibility of it shifting to closer ATM. The effect on Delta is that a higher volatility will flatten the Delta curve out more, so the delta for OTM will increase, and the delta for ITM will decrease (thought deep ITM/OTM will see a smaller effect). Interest Rates

The interest rate determines the cost of hedging, as shorting or buying shares requires interest (short has interest cost, buying shares means borrowing money to buy), which means if rates go up, then the cost of delta hedging will increase, and so the cost of the option increases.
